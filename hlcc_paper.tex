\begin{abstract}
We introduce the Hybrid Linear-Convex Confidence (\hlcc{}) loss, a
scoring function that combines a linear reward for correct predictions
with a quadratic penalty for confident errors.  Unlike proper scoring
rules such as the Brier score or log-loss, where the optimal confidence
equals the true accuracy ($\cstar = p$), \hlcc{} induces a conservative
mapping $\cstar = p/[4(1-p)]$ that naturally suppresses overconfidence
below 80\% accuracy.  We implement \hlcc{} as a trainable objective for
language-model confidence heads, proposing a dual-path
\emph{NormShift} architecture that routes per-layer normalisation
dynamics alongside final hidden states to estimate per-token confidence.
We benchmark against 11 baseline confidence methods---including maximum
softmax probability, entropy, self-consistency, semantic entropy,
chain-of-thought verbalisation, temperature scaling, isotonic
regression, and Platt scaling---across 9 datasets and 7 model scales
from 1.1B to 8B parameters.  Results are evaluated on discrimination
(AUROC, AUPRC), calibration (ECE, Brier), and selective prediction
(PRR, accuracy at coverage thresholds), providing a comprehensive
assessment of confidence estimation in modern language models.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% ============================================================

Reliable confidence estimation is essential for the safe deployment of
language models in high-stakes domains such as medicine, law, and
education.  Standard autoregressive training with cross-entropy loss
maximises token-level likelihood but provides no explicit training signal
for calibrated uncertainty~\cite{guo2017calibration}.  The resulting
softmax probabilities are often poorly calibrated, with models exhibiting
systematic overconfidence on out-of-distribution
inputs~\cite{hendrycks2017baseline}.

Proper scoring rules---the Brier score and logarithmic loss---are the
classical tools for eliciting calibrated probabilities.  Both are
\emph{strictly proper}: the expected score is maximised only when the
reported confidence equals the true probability of correctness.  However,
this mathematical elegance creates a symmetric treatment of over- and
under-confidence that may be inappropriate when the costs of confident
errors far exceed the costs of cautious under-reporting.

In confidence-based marking (CBM) for educational
assessment~\cite{gardnermedwin1995}, the asymmetry between ``confident
and wrong'' versus ``cautious and right'' is encoded through discrete
penalty scales.  Inspired by this asymmetry, we propose the Hybrid
Linear-Convex Confidence (\hlcc{}) scoring function:
\begin{equation}
  S(y, c) =
  \begin{cases}
    1 + c    & \text{if } y = 1 \text{ (correct)}, \\
    -2c^{2}  & \text{if } y = 0 \text{ (incorrect)},
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:hlcc-scoring}
\end{equation}
where $c \in [0,1]$ is the stated confidence and $y \in \{0,1\}$
indicates correctness.  The linear reward provides a constant marginal
incentive for increased confidence on correct predictions, while the
quadratic penalty creates an escalating marginal cost for confident
errors.  This structure naturally induces conservative confidence
estimates without requiring post-hoc recalibration.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item We derive the theoretical properties of \hlcc{}, showing that
        the optimal confidence $\cstar = p/[4(1-p)]$ is strictly below
        the true accuracy $p$ for $p < 0.75$, providing a built-in
        conservative bias (\Cref{sec:theory}).
  \item We propose the \emph{NormShift} confidence head, a dual-path
        architecture that combines per-layer normalisation shift signals
        with final hidden states to estimate token-level confidence
        (\Cref{sec:architecture}).
  \item We conduct a comprehensive benchmarking study covering 11
        baselines, 9 datasets, and 7 model scales with metrics spanning
        discrimination, calibration, and selective prediction
        (\Cref{sec:methodology,sec:results}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Theoretical Framework}
\label{sec:theory}
% ============================================================

\subsection{The HLCC Scoring Function}

\begin{definition}[HLCC Score]
  For a binary outcome $y \in \{0,1\}$ and stated confidence
  $c \in [0,1]$, the \hlcc{} score is
  \begin{equation}
    S(y, c) = y(1 + c) + (1-y)(-2c^{2}).
  \end{equation}
\end{definition}

The reward function $R(c) = 1 + c$ is affine with constant derivative
$R'(c) = 1$.  The penalty function $P(c) = -2c^{2}$ is convex with
derivative $P'(c) = -4c$, creating an escalating marginal cost.  At
$c = 0$, the score is $S(1,0) = 1$ and $S(0,0) = 0$, establishing a
\emph{safe guess} baseline: answering at zero confidence is always
weakly dominant over abstention.

\subsection{Optimal Confidence}

\begin{proposition}[Optimal Confidence under HLCC]
\label{prop:optimal}
  For an agent with true probability of correctness $p \in [0,1]$, the
  confidence $\cstar$ maximising expected \hlcc{} score is
  \begin{equation}
    \cstar = \min\!\left(1,\;\frac{p}{4(1-p)}\right).
    \label{eq:cstar}
  \end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  The expected score as a function of stated confidence $c$ is
  \begin{equation}
    \E[S] = p(1 + c) + (1-p)(-2c^{2}) = p + pc - 2(1-p)c^{2}.
  \end{equation}
  Differentiating with respect to $c$:
  \begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \E[S]}{\partial c} = p - 4(1-p)c.
  \end{equation}
  Setting to zero and solving for $c$:
  \begin{equation}
    \cstar = \frac{p}{4(1-p)},
  \end{equation}
  subject to the constraint $c \in [0,1]$, giving the minimum with~$1$.
  The second derivative $\partial^{2}\E[S]/\partial c^{2} = -4(1-p) < 0$
  for $p < 1$, confirming this is a maximum.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Crossover Point]
\label{cor:crossover}
  The \hlcc{} optimal confidence equals the true accuracy ($\cstar = p$)
  at exactly $p = 0.75$.  For $p < 0.75$, $\cstar < p$ (conservative).
  For $p > 0.75$, $\cstar > p$ until saturation at $\cstar = 1$ when
  $p \geq 0.8$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
  Setting $\cstar = p$: $p = p/[4(1-p)]$ implies $4(1-p) = 1$, hence
  $p = 3/4$.  The saturation point solves $p/[4(1-p)] = 1$, giving
  $p = 4/5$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Comparison with Proper Scoring Rules}

\hlcc{} is \emph{not} a strictly proper scoring rule.  Unlike the Brier
score $S_B = -(c-y)^{2}$ and log-loss
$S_L = y\log c + (1-y)\log(1-c)$, which are both maximised at $\cstar = p$,
\hlcc{} imposes a conservative transformation of accuracy to confidence.

\begin{proposition}[Reward--Penalty Asymmetry]
  At any confidence level $c > 0$, the marginal penalty for an incorrect
  prediction exceeds the marginal reward for a correct one:
  \begin{equation}
    |P'(c)| = 4c > 1 = R'(c) \quad \text{for all } c > 0.25.
  \end{equation}
  The marginal risk-to-reward ratio is $4c : 1$.
\end{proposition}

This asymmetry is the key structural difference from proper scoring
rules.  At $c = 1$, the risk-to-reward ratio is $4:1$ (marginal) or
$2:1$ (absolute: gaining 1 point from $R(0)=1$ to $R(1)=2$, but
risking a 2-point loss from $P(0)=0$ to $P(1)=-2$).

\subsection{Safe Guess Mechanism}

At $c = 0$, the score is $S(1,0) = 1$ (correct) and $S(0,0) = 0$
(incorrect).  The expected value of a random guess ($p = 0.25$ for
4-option MCQ) at $c = 0$ is $0.25 \times 1 = 0.25 > 0$.  This ensures
that participating is always dominant over abstention, resolving the
\emph{omission bias} observed in negative-marking schemes where
risk-averse test-takers skip questions they partially
know~\cite{budescu2012optimal}.

\section{Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}
% ============================================================

\subsection{NormShift Confidence Head}

We propose the \emph{NormShift} confidence head, a lightweight module
trained on top of a frozen language model to predict per-token
confidence.  The architecture exploits an observation about transformer
normalisation layers: the magnitude of the correction applied by
LayerNorm encodes information about the model's internal uncertainty.

\begin{definition}[Norm-Shift Signal]
  For layer $\ell$ of a transformer with LayerNorm producing output
  $\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}$ from pre-normalisation input
  $\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}$, the norm-shift signal is
  \begin{equation}
    \delta^{(\ell)} = \|\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}\|_{2} - \|\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}\|_{2},
  \end{equation}
  measuring how much the normalisation operation altered the
  representation magnitude.
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Dual-Path Architecture.}
The NormShift head processes two complementary information streams:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Path A} (Norm-Shift Path): The vector
        $\boldsymbol{\delta} = [\delta^{(1)}, \ldots, \delta^{(L)}] \in \R^{L}$
        of per-layer norm-shift signals is projected through a linear
        layer with GELU activation to an intermediate representation of
        dimension $d_{\text{mid}} = \max(64, 2L)$.
  \item \textbf{Path B} (Hidden-State Path): The final hidden state
        $\mathbf{h}^{(L)} \in \R^{d}$ is projected through a separate
        linear layer with GELU activation to the same intermediate
        dimension $d_{\text{mid}}$.
\end{itemize}

The two paths are concatenated and processed by a combination network:
\begin{equation}
  c = \sigma\!\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\,\mathbf{w}^{\top}
  \text{GELU}\!\left(\mathbf{W}_{\text{comb}}
  \begin{bmatrix} f_{A}(\boldsymbol{\delta}) \\ f_{B}(\mathbf{h}^{(L)}) \end{bmatrix}
  + \mathbf{b}_{\text{comb}}\right)\right),
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is a learnable temperature parameter initialised to 1, and
$\sigma$ is the sigmoid function.  The total parameter count is
approximately $2 \times d \times d_{\text{mid}} + 4d_{\text{mid}}^{2}$,
which for a 7B model ($d = 3584$, $L = 28$) is roughly 500K
parameters---negligible compared to the base model.

\paragraph{Training Objective.}
The confidence head is trained with the \hlcc{} loss from
\Cref{eq:hlcc-scoring}, combined with a cross-entropy warmup:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} + (1-\alpha)\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{HLCC}},
  \label{eq:combined-loss}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ decays from 1 to a target value over a warmup period.
The cross-entropy component stabilises early training before the
confidence head has learned meaningful representations.

\subsection{Uncertainty-Aware LayerNorm}

To capture the doubt signals, we extend standard LayerNorm to record
pre-normalisation statistics:

\begin{equation}
  \text{UncertaintyAwareLayerNorm}(\mathbf{x}) = \text{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{x}),
  \quad \text{storing} \quad
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \|\mathbf{x}\|_{2} \\
    \sqrt{\text{Var}(\mathbf{x}) + \epsilon} \\
    \text{Var}(\mathbf{x})
  \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

These three signals per layer---pre-norm magnitude, correction scale,
and variance---form the input to the norm-shift path.  For inference
with a pre-trained frozen model, only the norm-shift signals from
\texttt{output\_hidden\_states=True} are needed; no modification to the
base model is required.

\subsection{Confidence Methods}

We evaluate 12 confidence estimation methods organised in three tiers by
computational cost.

\paragraph{Tier 1: Single Forward Pass.}
These methods extract confidence from a single model forward pass and
add negligible overhead.

\begin{description}[nosep,leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item[Entropy] Shannon entropy of the softmax distribution over
        answer-choice tokens, normalised to $[0,1]$.
  \item[Top-$k$] Ratio of top-1 to top-2 logit probabilities.
  \item[MSP] Maximum softmax probability over choice
        tokens~\cite{hendrycks2017baseline}.
  \item[G-NLL] Greedy negative log-likelihood of the selected token.
  \item[NormShift (\hlcc{})] Trained dual-path confidence head using
        norm-shift signals and \hlcc{} loss (ours).
\end{description}

\paragraph{Tier 2: Post-Hoc Recalibration.}
These methods apply a learned transformation to a base confidence signal
(entropy) using a held-out calibration set.

\begin{description}[nosep,leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item[Temperature Scaling] Single learned temperature parameter
        $T$~\cite{guo2017calibration}.
  \item[Isotonic Regression] Non-parametric monotonic recalibration
        using the pool-adjacent-violators
        algorithm~\cite{zadrozny2002transforming}.
  \item[Platt Scaling] Two-parameter logistic regression
        $\sigma(a \cdot \text{logit}(c) + b)$~\cite{platt1999probabilistic}.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Tier 3: Multi-Pass Generation.}
These methods require multiple forward passes or extended generation per
example, with substantially higher compute cost.

\begin{description}[nosep,leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item[Verbalized] Prompt the model to self-report confidence as a
        number.
  \item[Self-Consistency] Sample $n=10$ answers and take the majority
        vote frequency as confidence~\cite{wang2023selfconsistency}.
  \item[Semantic Entropy] Cluster $n$ sampled answers by semantic
        equivalence and compute Shannon entropy over cluster
        probabilities~\cite{kuhn2023semantic}.
  \item[CoT Confidence] Two-stage prompting: generate chain-of-thought
        reasoning, then extract answer and confidence from a structured
        JSON response.
\end{description}

Additionally, we evaluate two combination methods:
\begin{description}[nosep,leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item[LVU] Linguistic Verification of Uncertainty: generate an
        explanation and detect hedging language (``might'', ``perhaps'')
        vs.\ certainty language (``definitely'', ``clearly'').
  \item[CoCoA] Confidence Combination Approach: weighted average of
        entropy and self-consistency signals.
\end{description}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We report metrics across three complementary axes.

\paragraph{Discrimination.}
Can the confidence score distinguish correct from incorrect predictions?

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{AUROC}: Area under the ROC curve, treating
        $\text{confidence} > t$ as a binary classifier for correctness.
  \item \textbf{AUPRC}: Area under the precision-recall curve, more
        informative than AUROC when the class distribution is
        imbalanced~\cite{saito2015precision}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Calibration.}
Does the confidence match the empirical frequency of correctness?

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{ECE}: Expected Calibration Error with 10 equal-width
        bins~\cite{naeini2015obtaining}.
  \item \textbf{Brier Score}: Mean squared error between confidence and
        binary correctness, $\frac{1}{n}\sum(c_i - y_i)^{2}$.
  \item \textbf{Overconfidence Rate}: Fraction of incorrect predictions
        with confidence $> 0.7$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Selective Prediction.}
How does accuracy improve when the model abstains on low-confidence
examples?

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{PRR}: Prediction Rejection Ratio.  Measures the area
        between the model's accuracy-vs-rejection curve and the random
        baseline, normalised by the oracle upper
        bound~\cite{malinin2017predictive}:
        \begin{equation}
          \text{PRR} = \frac{\text{AULC}_{\text{random}} -
          \text{AULC}_{\text{model}}}{\text{AULC}_{\text{random}} -
          \text{AULC}_{\text{oracle}}}.
        \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Accuracy@Coverage}: Accuracy on the top $k$\% most
        confident predictions, reported at $k \in \{70, 80, 90\}$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{HLCC-Specific.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{HLCC Score}: Mean score under
        \Cref{eq:hlcc-scoring}, measuring confidence-weighted
        performance.
  \item \textbf{Calibration Gap}: $|\bar{c} - \text{accuracy}|$, the
        absolute difference between mean confidence and overall accuracy.
  \item \textbf{CBM Score}: Score under the discrete CBM scheme for
        comparison with educational assessment literature.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
% ============================================================

\paragraph{Hardware.}
All experiments run on a single NVIDIA RTX 3070 Laptop GPU (8\,GB
VRAM), Intel i7-10870H CPU (8 cores / 16 threads), and 32\,GB RAM.
Models are loaded in 4-bit NF4 quantisation with double quantisation
to fit within the VRAM budget.

\paragraph{MCQ Evaluation Protocol.}
For each (model, dataset) pair, we evaluate up to 300 examples.  The
base forward pass computes choice-token logits shared across all Tier~1
methods.  Tier~2 methods use a 50/50 calibration/test split.  Tier~3
methods use $n = 10$ samples for self-consistency and semantic entropy.

\paragraph{NormShift Training.}
The confidence head is trained for 25 epochs on MCQ data with the
combined loss from \Cref{eq:combined-loss}, using $\alpha = 0.5$ and a
500-step cross-entropy warmup.  The base model is frozen; only the
confidence head parameters ($\sim$500K) are updated.  We use AdamW with
learning rate $3 \times 10^{-4}$ and weight decay $0.01$.  Two variants
are trained per model:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Combined}: Full dual-path architecture (norm-shift +
        hidden state).
  \item \textbf{Norm-Shift Only}: Ablation using only the norm-shift
        path, to isolate the contribution of normalisation dynamics.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Multi-Day Orchestration.}
The full experiment suite is managed by an automated orchestrator with
crash recovery.  Each model progresses through stages: baseline MCQ
evaluation, confidence head training, calibration benchmarks (Tiers~1--3),
and extended dataset evaluation.  State is persisted to a JSON checkpoint
after each stage, enabling resumption after interruption.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

\paragraph{Conservative Bias as a Feature.}
The \hlcc{} optimal confidence formula $\cstar = p/[4(1-p)]$
systematically underestimates confidence for models with accuracy below
75\%.  While this departs from the strict propriety of Brier and
log-loss, it may be advantageous in deployment scenarios where
overconfident errors carry disproportionate cost---such as medical
diagnosis, legal reasoning, or autonomous decision-making.

\paragraph{Model-Dependent NormShift Effectiveness.}
The NormShift head exhibits striking performance variation across model
architectures.  On Llama-3.1-8B, NormShift achieves the best
calibration of any method (ECE\,=\,0.105 vs.\ 0.322 for
self-consistency) and competitive discrimination (AUROC\,=\,0.870
vs.\ 0.858 for entropy), using only a single forward pass.  On
Qwen2.5-7B, however, the same training procedure yields
AUROC\,=\,0.401---below random.  This suggests that the utility of
norm-shift signals as a confidence proxy varies substantially with the
model's internal normalisation dynamics and may require
architecture-specific investigation.

\paragraph{NormShift Signal Interpretation.}
The norm-shift signal $\delta^{(\ell)}$ captures how much each
transformer layer's normalisation step altered the representation.
Layers that apply large corrections may indicate inputs that fall outside
the model's learned distribution, providing a complementary uncertainty
signal to logit-based methods.  The ablation (\Cref{tab:ablation})
quantifies the contribution of this signal relative to the standard
hidden-state approach.

\paragraph{Computational Cost Tradeoffs.}
Tier~1 methods (entropy, MSP, NormShift) add negligible cost to
inference.  The NormShift head requires a single additional forward pass
through $\sim$500K parameters.  In contrast, Tier~3 methods
(self-consistency, semantic entropy) multiply inference cost by the
sample count $n$.  On Llama-3.1-8B, NormShift matches or exceeds
multi-pass methods at a fraction of the cost.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item All models are evaluated under 4-bit quantisation; full-precision
        results may differ.
  \item The MCQ evaluation format may not fully capture open-ended
        generation confidence.
  \item The NormShift head is trained per-model and does not transfer
        across architectures.
  \item Performance varies substantially across model architectures;
        Llama-3.1-8B shows excellent results while Qwen2.5-7B shows
        poor discrimination, suggesting architecture-dependent
        factors.
  \item The HLCC scoring function's conservative bias may undervalue
        confidence in well-calibrated high-accuracy models.
\end{enumerate}

\section{LM-Polygraph Integration}
\label{sec:polygraph}
% ============================================================

To enable direct comparison with the broader uncertainty estimation
literature, we implement the NormShift confidence head as a custom
estimator within the
LM-Polygraph~\cite{fadeeva2024lmpolygraph} benchmarking framework.
LM-Polygraph provides over 40 uncertainty estimation methods and
standardised evaluation protocols, making it the primary benchmark
for comparing UE methods across models and tasks.

\subsection{Integration Architecture}

The integration wraps the NormShift confidence head as an LM-Polygraph
\texttt{Estimator} subclass.  The key challenge is that NormShift
requires full hidden states from all transformer layers
(\texttt{output\_hidden\_states=True}), while most LM-Polygraph methods
operate only on output logits.

We implement a custom \texttt{StatCalculator} that extracts three
signals from each forward pass:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item The full hidden states tuple
        $({\mathbf{h}^{(0)}, \ldots, \mathbf{h}^{(L)}})$,
  \item Per-layer norm-shift signals
        $\boldsymbol{\delta} \in \R^{L}$ via
        $\delta^{(\ell)} = 1 - \text{std}(\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)})$,
  \item The final hidden state $\mathbf{h}^{(L)}$ for the combined path.
\end{enumerate}

The estimator inverts the confidence output to match LM-Polygraph's
convention (higher values indicate greater uncertainty):
\begin{equation}
  u_{\text{NormShift}} = 1 - c_{\text{NormShift}}.
\end{equation}

All models are loaded with the same 4-bit NF4 quantisation used
throughout this work, and evaluation uses identical MCQ prompts to
ensure comparability across methods.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ============================================================

\paragraph{Confidence Calibration in Neural Networks.}
\citet{guo2017calibration} demonstrated that modern neural networks are
poorly calibrated, with temperature scaling as a simple post-hoc fix.
\citet{naeini2015obtaining} introduced ECE as the standard calibration
metric.  \citet{nixon2019measuring} showed that ECE is sensitive to
binning choices and proposed adaptive calibration error.

\paragraph{Uncertainty Estimation in LLMs.}
\citet{kadavath2022language} studied self-evaluation in large language
models, finding that models can estimate their own accuracy.
\citet{kuhn2023semantic} proposed semantic entropy as a
generation-aware uncertainty measure.  \citet{xiong2024llms} provided a
comprehensive survey of LLM confidence estimation, categorising methods
into logit-based, verbalised, and consistency-based
approaches.  \citet{lin2024generating} and \citet{tian2023just} explored
verbalized confidence elicitation and found that prompting strategies
significantly affect self-reported confidence quality.

\paragraph{Selective Prediction.}
\citet{geifman2017selective} formalised selective prediction as
accuracy-coverage tradeoffs.  The prediction rejection ratio (PRR) was
introduced by \citet{malinin2017predictive} and adopted by the
LM-Polygraph framework~\cite{fadeeva2024lmpolygraph} as a standard
metric for uncertainty benchmarking.

\paragraph{Confidence-Based Marking.}
\citet{gardnermedwin1995} introduced the CBM scheme for educational
assessment.  \citet{budescu2012optimal} studied optimal scoring rules
for confidence-weighted testing, analysing the tradeoff between strict
propriety and behavioural desiderata such as participation incentives.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

We have presented the \hlcc{} scoring function and the NormShift
confidence head as a method for training calibrated confidence estimates
in language models.  Our key findings are:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{In-domain dominance.}  When trained on the target
        distribution, NormShift achieves AUROC\,=\,0.752 and
        PRR\,=\,0.484 on TruthfulQA---surpassing all logit-based
        methods (best: top-$k$ at 0.634 / 0.177) while requiring only
        a single forward pass through $\sim$500K additional parameters.
  \item \textbf{Architecture sensitivity.}  On Llama-3.1-8B, NormShift
        achieves state-of-the-art calibration (ECE\,=\,0.105) and
        discrimination (AUROC\,=\,0.870) even with an out-of-domain
        checkpoint, while Qwen2.5-7B requires in-domain training.
  \item \textbf{Limited transfer.}  Cross-dataset evaluation reveals
        that NormShift degrades on out-of-distribution data
        ($\Delta$AUROC\,$= -0.26$ to $-0.32$), motivating future work
        on domain-agnostic norm-shift features.
  \item \textbf{Conservative bias.}  The \hlcc{} scoring function
        induces a conservative confidence mapping ($\cstar < p$ for
        $p < 0.75$), which heavily penalises overconfident errors.
        This makes NormShift the only single-pass method achieving
        positive HLCC scores on Llama-3.1-8B, where all logit-based
        methods score negative due to severe overconfidence.
\end{enumerate}

The theoretical analysis confirms that \hlcc{} departs from strict
propriety in favour of asymmetric risk---rewarding correct confidence
linearly but penalising incorrect confidence quadratically.  Our
LM-Polygraph integration enables direct comparison with the broader
uncertainty estimation literature, and our benchmarking framework
provides a systematic evaluation methodology for future confidence
estimation research.
