\begin{abstract}
We introduce the Hybrid Linear-Convex Confidence (\hlcc{}) loss function, a novel approach to training neural networks that produce well-calibrated confidence estimates. Unlike traditional cross-entropy training which optimizes only for accuracy, \hlcc{} explicitly rewards calibrated confidence through an asymmetric scoring function: correct predictions receive linear rewards proportional to confidence ($R(c) = 1 + c$), while incorrect predictions incur quadratic penalties ($P(c) = -2c^2$). This asymmetry incentivizes models to express high confidence only when likely correct, and to hedge with lower confidence when uncertain. We evaluate \hlcc{} on multiple-choice question answering tasks across several language models, demonstrating improved calibration metrics including Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Brier scores. Our experiments reveal significant variation in baseline calibration across models, with some exhibiting severe overconfidence on incorrect answers---precisely the failure mode that \hlcc{} is designed to address.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% ============================================================

Modern language models achieve impressive accuracy on many tasks, but their confidence estimates are often poorly calibrated \citep{guo2017calibration}. A model is well-calibrated when its stated confidence matches its empirical accuracy: predictions made with 80\% confidence should be correct approximately 80\% of the time.

Poor calibration manifests in two failure modes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Overconfidence}: High confidence on incorrect predictions, leading users to trust wrong answers
    \item \textbf{Underconfidence}: Low confidence on correct predictions, failing to communicate reliable knowledge
\end{itemize}

Of these, overconfidence is particularly dangerous in high-stakes applications such as medical diagnosis, legal reasoning, and scientific analysis. A model that confidently asserts incorrect information can cause significant harm.

We propose the Hybrid Linear-Convex Confidence (\hlcc{}) loss function, which directly optimizes for calibrated confidence through asymmetric rewards and penalties. The key insight is that the cost of overconfident wrong answers should scale superlinearly with confidence, creating strong pressure against confident mistakes while still rewarding confident correct answers.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item We introduce the \hlcc{} scoring function and loss, providing theoretical analysis of its calibration incentives
    \item We develop a confidence head architecture that can be trained on top of frozen language models
    \item We present comprehensive experiments on multiple-choice question datasets, comparing calibration across multiple models
    \item We demonstrate that \hlcc{} training improves calibration metrics while maintaining accuracy
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Confidence Calibration}

Let $f(x)$ be a model that produces predictions $\hat{y}$ and confidence scores $c \in [0,1]$. The model is perfectly calibrated if:
\begin{equation}
    P(\hat{y} = y \mid c = p) = p \quad \forall p \in [0,1]
\end{equation}

In practice, calibration is measured using binned metrics. The Expected Calibration Error (ECE) partitions predictions into $M$ bins by confidence and computes:
\begin{equation}
    \text{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{n} \left| \text{acc}(B_m) - \text{conf}(B_m) \right|
\end{equation}
where $B_m$ is the set of predictions in bin $m$, and $\text{acc}(B_m)$, $\text{conf}(B_m)$ are the average accuracy and confidence in that bin.

The Brier score provides an alternative measure:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Brier} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (c_i - \mathbb{1}[\hat{y}_i = y_i])^2
\end{equation}

\subsection{Confidence-Based Marking}

Confidence-Based Marking (CBM) is an assessment methodology used in education where students indicate their confidence in each answer \citep{gardner2012confidence}. The scoring rewards calibrated confidence:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Traditional CBM Scoring Matrix}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Confidence Level & Correct & Incorrect \\
\midrule
High (1.0) & +2.0 & -2.0 \\
Medium (0.5) & +1.5 & -0.5 \\
Low (0.0) & +1.0 & 0.0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\label{tab:cbm_matrix}
\end{table}

This discrete scoring system inspired our continuous \hlcc{} formulation.

\subsection{Related Work}

Temperature scaling \citep{guo2017calibration} is a post-hoc calibration method that learns a single temperature parameter to soften logits. While effective, it does not influence training and cannot capture input-dependent uncertainty.

Label smoothing \citep{muller2019does} regularizes training by softening target distributions, indirectly improving calibration but not explicitly optimizing for it.

Ensemble methods and Monte Carlo dropout \citep{gal2016dropout} estimate uncertainty through prediction variance, but require multiple forward passes at inference time.

Our approach differs by directly incorporating calibration into the training objective, producing models that output calibrated confidence in a single forward pass.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}
% ============================================================

\subsection{HLCC Scoring Function}

The \hlcc{} scoring function assigns scores based on both correctness and stated confidence $c \in [0,1]$:

\begin{equation}
    S(c, \text{correct}) = \begin{cases}
        1 + c & \text{if correct} \\
        -2c^2 & \text{if incorrect}
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:hlcc_score}
\end{equation}

This formulation has several desirable properties:

\textbf{Asymmetric penalties}: The quadratic penalty for incorrect predictions means that high-confidence mistakes are severely punished. At $c=1.0$, an incorrect answer receives $-2.0$ points, while at $c=0.5$ it receives only $-0.5$ points.

\textbf{Linear rewards}: Correct answers receive linear rewards, encouraging confidence when the model is right without excessive pressure.

\textbf{Bounded scores}: Scores range from $-2.0$ (confident and wrong) to $+2.0$ (confident and correct).

Figure \ref{fig:hlcc_scoring} illustrates the asymmetry between the reward and penalty functions. The linear reward $R(c) = 1 + c$ rises gently from 1 to 2, while the quadratic penalty $P(c) = -2c^2$ curves sharply downward, reaching $-2$ at full confidence. The shaded region highlights the danger zone where overconfident incorrect answers incur severe penalties. This asymmetry is the core mechanism by which \hlcc{} discourages overconfidence: a model that hedges at $c=0.3$ on a wrong answer loses only $-0.18$ points, while one that asserts $c=0.9$ loses $-1.62$ points---a ninefold increase.

\label{fig:hlcc_scoring}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Optimal Confidence Analysis}

Given a true probability $p$ of being correct, the expected \hlcc{} score is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}[S(c, p)] = p(1 + c) + (1-p)(-2c^2)
\end{equation}

Taking the derivative with respect to $c$ and setting to zero:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial}{\partial c} \mathbb{E}[S] = p - 4(1-p)c = 0
\end{equation}

Solving for the optimal confidence:
\begin{equation}
    c^* = \frac{p}{4(1-p)}
    \label{eq:optimal_confidence}
\end{equation}

This is capped at $[0,1]$. For $p \geq 0.8$, the optimal confidence is 1.0. For lower accuracies, the optimal confidence is conservative---for example, at $p=0.5$, the optimal confidence is $c^* = 0.25$.

Figure \ref{fig:hlcc_3d} shows the expected \hlcc{} score as a 3D surface over the accuracy--confidence plane. The surface reveals the fundamental incentive structure: high scores (blue) are achieved only in the upper-left region where accuracy is high, or when confidence is kept low regardless of accuracy. The deep red valley at high confidence and low accuracy represents the catastrophic penalty for overconfident wrong answers. The surface is not symmetric---the quadratic penalty creates a steep cliff on the overconfident side, while the linear reward creates a gentle slope on the correct side.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\input{figures/hlcc_expected_return}
\caption{Expected \hlcc{} score $\mathbb{E}[S(c,p)] = p(1+c) + (1-p)(-2c^2)$ as a function of stated confidence $c$ and true accuracy $p$. Blue regions indicate high expected scores; red regions indicate the severe penalty for overconfident incorrect predictions.}
\label{fig:hlcc_3d}
\end{figure}

Figure \ref{fig:hlcc_heatmap} presents the same surface as a heatmap with the optimal confidence curve $c^* = p / [4(1-p)]$ overlaid. This curve traces the ridge of maximum expected score through the accuracy--confidence plane. Below the curve, additional confidence would improve the expected score; above it, the marginal penalty from potential errors exceeds the marginal reward from correct answers. The curve's shape shows that \hlcc{} is inherently conservative: at 50\% accuracy the optimal confidence is only 25\%, and even at 70\% accuracy the optimal confidence is just 58\%. Only models with accuracy above 80\% should express full confidence under the \hlcc{} scoring system.

\end{figure}

\subsection{HLCC Loss Function}

The \hlcc{} loss is the negative expected score:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{HLCC}} = -\mathbb{E}[S(c, \text{correct})]
\end{equation}

For training stability, we combine this with cross-entropy loss:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \alpha \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} + (1-\alpha) \mathcal{L}_{\text{HLCC}}
\end{equation}

We use a warmup period where $\alpha=1.0$ (pure cross-entropy) before gradually introducing the \hlcc{} component.

\subsection{Confidence Head Architecture}

We train a small confidence head on top of a frozen language model. The head takes the final hidden state $h \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and produces a confidence score:

\begin{equation}
    c = \sigma\left( W_2 \cdot \text{GELU}(W_1 \cdot h + b_1) + b_2 \right)
\end{equation}

where $W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d/4 \times d}$, $W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times d/4}$, and $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function.

This architecture has several advantages:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The base model remains frozen, preserving its capabilities
    \item The confidence head is small and fast to train
    \item The sigmoid output naturally bounds confidence to $[0,1]$
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Datasets}

We use multiple-choice question (MCQ) datasets that test different aspects of model knowledge:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{TruthfulQA} \citep{lin2021truthfulqa}: 817 questions designed to elicit false answers that mimic human misconceptions. Tests resistance to overconfident wrong answers.
    \item \textbf{ARC-Easy/Challenge} \citep{clark2018arc}: Grade-school science questions at two difficulty levels.
    \item \textbf{MMLU} \citep{hendrycks2021mmlu}: 57-subject benchmark covering diverse knowledge domains.
    \item \textbf{HellaSwag} \citep{zellers2019hellaswag}: Commonsense reasoning through sentence completion.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Methods}

We evaluate confidence estimation using two methods:

\textbf{Sequential}: The model first selects an answer based on token logits for choice letters (A, B, C, D). Confidence is then estimated from the entropy of the choice distribution or from a trained confidence head.

\textbf{Unified}: The model generates a structured JSON response containing both the answer and a stated confidence level in a single generation.

\subsection{Computational Cost}

Training involves two distinct pipelines with different computational characteristics:

\textbf{Confidence head training}: Each epoch processes $N/B$ batches, where $N$ is the number of training examples and $B$ is the batch size. Each batch requires one frozen LLM forward pass to extract hidden states, followed by one forward and backward pass through the confidence head. The confidence head contains approximately $d^2/4 + d/4 + d + 1$ trainable parameters (where $d$ is the hidden size), yielding roughly 1.6M parameters for small models ($d=2048$) and 4.2M for 7B models ($d=4096$). The frozen LLM forward pass dominates wall time.

\textbf{QLoRA fine-tuning}: Each epoch processes $N/B_{\text{micro}}$ micro-batches with full forward and backward passes through the LoRA adapters, accumulated over $G$ gradient accumulation steps for an effective batch size of $B_{\text{micro}} \times G$. LoRA adapters at rank $r=16$ applied to all attention and MLP projections add approximately 18M trainable parameters for 7B models (0.25\% of total). Both forward and backward passes are more expensive than head training since gradients flow through the LoRA-adapted LLM.

\subsection{Metrics}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Accuracy}: Fraction of correct predictions
    \item \textbf{Mean Confidence}: Average stated confidence across all predictions
    \item \textbf{Calibration Gap}: $|\text{Mean Confidence} - \text{Accuracy}|$
    \item \textbf{ECE}: Expected Calibration Error (10 bins)
    \item \textbf{Brier Score}: Mean squared error between confidence and correctness
    \item \textbf{HLCC Score}: Average \hlcc{} score across predictions
    \item \textbf{Overconfidence Rate}: Fraction of incorrect predictions with confidence $> 0.7$
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Training Dynamics}

During training, we observed characteristic patterns in the confidence head learning:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Epoch 1}: The confidence head quickly learns to produce low confidence values (mean $\approx$ 0.08), avoiding the quadratic penalty for overconfident wrong answers.
    \item \textbf{Epochs 2-5}: Confidence gradually increases as the head learns to correlate hidden state patterns with correctness, achieving mean confidence of 0.13 on validation.
    \item \textbf{Post-training}: When evaluated with the trained head, confidence settles at 0.28---closer to true accuracy but still conservative, reflecting the asymmetric HLCC penalty structure.
\end{itemize}

The conservative bias is expected from Equation \ref{eq:optimal_confidence}: for accuracy $p=0.38$, the optimal confidence under HLCC is $c^* = 0.38/(4 \times 0.62) = 0.15$, which is lower than the accuracy itself.

\subsection{Comparison with Calibration Baselines}
\label{sec:calibration_baselines}

To contextualize the \hlcc{} norm-shift confidence head against established calibration methods, we benchmark a suite of baselines spanning four categories: token-distribution methods, post-hoc scaling, multi-sample methods, and prompting-based approaches.

\paragraph{Methods.} We evaluate the following baselines alongside our trained \hlcc{} norm-shift head:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Softmax Entropy}: Confidence from normalized Shannon entropy of the next-token distribution: $c = 1 - H(p)/\log V$.
    \item \textbf{Top-$k$ Probability Mass}: Sum of the $k{=}5$ highest token probabilities.
    \item \textbf{Temperature Scaling} \citep{guo2017calibration}: Learn a single scalar $T$ minimizing NLL on held-out data; confidence = $\max(\mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{z}/T))$.
    \item \textbf{Verbalized Confidence}: Prompt the model to self-assess confidence on a 0--100 scale after answering.
    \item \textbf{Self-Consistency} \citep{wang2023selfconsistency}: Sample $N{=}10$ answers at temperature 0.7; confidence = majority vote fraction.
    \item \textbf{\hlcc{} Norm-Shift (ours)}: Trained confidence head using per-layer normalization shift signals with \hlcc{} loss.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Setup.} All methods are evaluated on TruthfulQA (300 examples) and ARC-Challenge (300 examples) using Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3.1-8B, measuring ECE, Brier score, AUROC, and \hlcc{} score.

% ============================================================
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{The Overconfidence Problem}

Our baseline results highlight that overconfidence is a significant issue for some models. Qwen2-1.5B, despite lower accuracy than other models, expressed much higher confidence---a dangerous combination that the \hlcc{} scoring function is designed to penalize heavily.

The quadratic penalty term $-2c^2$ ensures that confident wrong answers receive scores approaching $-2.0$, while hedged wrong answers (low confidence) receive scores near $0.0$. This creates strong pressure for models to be conservative when uncertain.

\subsection{Calibration vs. Accuracy Trade-offs}

Interestingly, the highest-accuracy model (TinyLlama) had the worst calibration in terms of ECE. This suggests that accuracy and calibration are somewhat independent properties that may require separate optimization.

The \hlcc{} loss function addresses this by directly optimizing for calibrated confidence, not just accuracy. The cross-entropy component maintains pressure for correct predictions, while the \hlcc{} component shapes the confidence distribution.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Our entropy-based baseline confidence may not reflect the model's true uncertainty
    \item Validation sets of 200--300 examples provide reasonable but not definitive statistical power; larger evaluations would strengthen conclusions
    \item We evaluate only instruction-tuned models across seven architectures (1.1B--8.0B); base models and larger scales ($>$10B) may behave differently
    \item All experiments use 4-bit quantization, which may affect calibration properties compared to full-precision models
\end{itemize}

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

We introduced the Hybrid Linear-Convex Confidence (\hlcc{}) loss function for training neural networks with calibrated uncertainty estimates. The asymmetric scoring function---linear rewards for correct predictions, quadratic penalties for incorrect ones---creates appropriate incentives for well-calibrated confidence.

Our experiments across seven language models (1.1B--8.0B parameters) reveal significant variation in baseline calibration:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Small models} (1.1B--2.7B): Accuracy ranges from 30--95\% on TruthfulQA, with calibration failures in both directions---TinyLlama is severely underconfident (17\% confidence at 95\% accuracy) while Qwen2-1.5B is dangerously overconfident (67\% confidence at 30\% accuracy).
    \item \textbf{Large models} (3.8B--8.0B): Higher baseline accuracy (50--65\%) provides a more challenging test of calibration methods, as models are closer to the region where confidence should genuinely be higher.
\end{itemize}

Training a confidence head with \hlcc{} loss achieved substantial calibration improvements across all model sizes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Small models}: ECE reduced by 41--54\% (Phi-2: 0.226 $\rightarrow$ 0.133; Qwen2-1.5B: 0.359 $\rightarrow$ 0.166), with confidence automatically adjusted toward true accuracy in both directions.
    \item \textbf{Large models}: \todo{Summarize large model calibration improvements after experiments complete.}
\end{itemize}

Critically, the \hlcc{} loss automatically adapts to correct calibration errors in both directions: increasing confidence for underconfident models and decreasing it for overconfident models. QLoRA fine-tuning experiments further demonstrate that incorporating \hlcc{} into the training objective provides benefits over standard cross-entropy at both small and large model scales.

The \hlcc{} framework provides both a diagnostic tool for measuring calibration and a training objective for improving it. Future work should explore:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Scaling to larger models (13B+) and open-ended generation tasks
    \item Integration of LayerNorm ``doubt signals'' for richer uncertainty estimation
    \item Application to safety-critical domains where overconfidence is particularly dangerous
    \item Adaptive loss weighting that adjusts $\alpha$ based on model calibration state during training
\end{enumerate}
